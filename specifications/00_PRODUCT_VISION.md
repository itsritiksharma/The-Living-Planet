# The Living Planet — Product Vision and Enduring Context

## 1. One-sentence concept

**The Living Planet** is a shared-world ecological civilization simulation in which players pursue different goals while the combined consequences of their decisions reshape a finite planet.

## 2. Product premise

The game is more than a survival game, city builder, climate-awareness quiz, or resource-management dashboard. It simulates how people, communities, economies, institutions, technologies, and ecosystems interact over time.

Players can pursue comfort, wealth, consumption, status, industrial growth, technological progress, conservation, cooperation, or philosophical inquiry. The game does not need to assign a single moral label to a player. Instead, it should show the effects of decisions within a connected system.

The central design statement is:

> Individually reasonable or rewarding choices can collectively create a catastrophe, and changing a system becomes harder when people, institutions, and livelihoods depend on it.

The game should help players experience:
- Delayed consequences: a choice can be rewarding now and damaging later.
- Accumulation: pollution and ecological damage can build up over time.
- Feedback loops: scarcity can provoke behavior that worsens scarcity.
- Collective-action problems: a shared benefit may require each player to bear a cost.
- Unequal vulnerability: the same crisis affects communities differently.
- Institutional inertia: systems can reward harmful behavior even when individuals understand the risks.
- Agency: personal behavior matters, but infrastructure, power, institutions, and collective coordination matter too.
- Uncertainty: ecological outcomes are not always predictable or reversible.

## 3. Core design principles

### 3.1 No single villain
Catastrophe should emerge from interacting incentives, habits, institutions, choices, and constraints—not from one cartoonishly evil character.

### 3.2 Finite and differentiated resources
Some resources regenerate, some regenerate slowly, and some are effectively nonrenewable at human timescales. Extraction and regeneration must be represented separately.

### 3.3 Consequences, not lectures
The simulation should make consequences visible through the world and its history. Do not substitute repetitive moralizing dialogue for actual systems.

### 3.4 No mandatory ideology
Players can make different choices. The physics and ecological rules should not change to flatter a player's beliefs.

### 3.5 No guaranteed happy ending
Some scenarios may end in severe decline or collapse. Good coordination should improve the odds, not guarantee success.

### 3.6 No simplistic individualism
A sustainable individual may still be harmed by a failing shared ecosystem. Conversely, personal and community action can influence larger systems.

### 3.7 Technology is neither magic nor useless
Clean energy, efficiency, recycling, restoration, adaptation, and research can help. Each has material requirements, costs, time delays, and limits. Efficiency may also create rebound effects if it makes consumption cheaper and encourages more use.

### 3.8 Ignorance and inaction matter
A player can lack information, lack access to information, avoid confronting known evidence, remain silent, or actively obstruct reform. These states are different and should not be treated as morally or mechanically identical.

### 3.9 Philosophy is inquiry, not a score
The AP-inspired layer should invite players to examine motivations and assumptions. It must not label players as enlightened or ignorant based on quiz answers.

## 4. Primary world systems

The long-term simulation may include:

1. **Natural resources:** freshwater, fertile soil, forests, fish, minerals, fossil fuels, and biodiversity.
2. **Climate and pollution:** greenhouse-gas emissions, atmospheric concentration, temperature, extreme weather, air quality, ocean health, and local pollution.
3. **Economy and consumption:** production, demand, jobs, trade, money, supply chains, inequality, infrastructure, and access to essentials.
4. **Society:** trust, cooperation, social norms, awareness, misinformation, political power, public support, and collective action.
5. **Technology and adaptation:** renewable energy, efficient buildings, public transport, water treatment, resilient agriculture, recycling, and carbon removal.
6. **Human well-being:** access to food, water, shelter, energy, safety, meaningful participation, and the distribution of burdens.

Do not put every system into the first prototype. Phase scope is defined in the phase documents.

## 5. Playable approaches, not fixed moral classes

Long-term play may support overlapping approaches:

- **Consumerism:** acquiring goods, convenience, novelty, and status.
- **Materialist growth:** industrial output, infrastructure, productivity, and measurable material progress.
- **Status and power:** influence, prestige, political control, and advantage over others.
- **Ecological cooperation:** stewardship, sharing, restoration, and long-term resilience.
- **Technological optimism:** research, invention, and engineering-led solutions.

These are behavioral tendencies or strategies, not locked character classes. One player may advocate sustainability while consuming heavily; another may operate an industry while improving its production methods and protecting workers.

The game should expose tensions between stated values and actual choices without automatically judging the player.

## 6. AP-framework-inspired inquiry

The game may draw inspiration from themes associated with the Acharya Prashant framework, including ignorance, desire, identification/ego, conditioning, fear, and self-inquiry. These themes should be presented as invitations to investigate—not as forced doctrine, authoritative diagnosis, or a requirement to agree with a teacher.

Potential questions:
- What problem am I trying to solve?
- What desire or fear is influencing this choice?
- What assumptions make this action appear necessary?
- Am I defending evidence or protecting an identity?
- Who benefits, and who bears the cost?
- Am I addressing a cause or only a symptom?
- Am I ignoring information because it threatens my interests?
- What would change if this habit or social norm were questioned?

Reflection must not grant magical ecological bonuses. Any benefit should arise through actual changed decisions, improved understanding, or better coordination.

## 7. Inaction and power

The game should distinguish:
- **Uninformed:** the player has not received or cannot access relevant information.
- **Uncertain:** the evidence is incomplete or contested.
- **Avoidant:** the player avoids evidence that threatens comfort, status, or identity.
- **Silent:** the player recognizes a problem but does not publicly support a response.
- **Power-constrained:** the player lacks resources or faces credible retaliation.
- **Obstructive:** the player actively blocks a response.
- **Supportive:** the player contributes evidence, money, labor, political backing, or organizing.

Inaction may preserve the status quo, but not everyone has equal power or equal freedom to act. Do not flatten these distinctions into a single morality meter.

## 8. Long-term vision

The game may eventually include:
- A persistent multiplayer settlement.
- Institutions, laws, negotiation, collective projects, misinformation, and social movements.
- More detailed economic and ecological systems.
- Several planets with distinct starting conditions.
- Costly exploration and settlement requiring research, energy, materials, and life-support infrastructure.
- Interplanetary migration that tests whether communities reproduce old patterns or learn from previous outcomes.
- Historical reports showing how choices, events, and feedback loops shaped the world.

These are not Phase 1 requirements.

## 9. Intended player experience

Players should:
1. Observe a world and its immediate needs.
2. Choose an action with visible short-term incentives.
3. Experience delayed economic, ecological, and social consequences.
4. Investigate why those consequences occurred.
5. Decide whether to repeat a pattern, change a habit, support a reform, or alter an institution.
6. See how the shared world responds over time.

## 10. Scope boundaries

This is a simulation and educational/reflective game, not a validated climate forecast. Any numeric values, thresholds, and outcomes used for gameplay must be documented as assumptions unless independently sourced and scientifically validated.

The game must not claim that:
- Every ecological process is exponential.
- All environmental thresholds are precisely known.
- Technology alone always solves the problem.
- individual behavior alone is sufficient to solve systemic problems.
- all societies inevitably collapse.
- a player's chosen philosophy guarantees a particular outcome.

The simulation should reflect uncertainty and differentiated causal relationships.
