# Phase 2 — Society, Institutions, and Philosophical Inquiry

## Purpose

Expand the prototype from a small resource loop into a social simulation. Begin only after Phase 1 is playable, tested, and stable.

## 1. Phase 2 goals

- Model social incentives and unequal power more clearly.
- Make collective action, silence, obstruction, and reform consequential.
- Introduce richer player goals without locking players into moral classes.
- Expand AP-inspired inquiry into contextual, optional moments.
- Show how individual decisions interact with infrastructure, markets, and institutions.
- Avoid reducing the simulation to a morality meter or a sequence of quizzes.

## 2. Social state candidates

Add only when they support gameplay:
- Community trust.
- Public awareness and access to reliable information.
- Inequality / distribution of resources.
- Institutional legitimacy.
- Reform capacity.
- Worker and household security.
- Political influence.
- Cooperation or coordination capacity.
- Misinformation prevalence, if the game can represent it responsibly.

Define each variable operationally. Avoid vague scores that change without understandable causes.

## 3. Behavior and play styles

Players may pursue overlapping strategies:
- Consumerism: comfort, novelty, goods, convenience, and status.
- Materialist growth: production, infrastructure, productivity, and output.
- Status/power: influence, prestige, control, and advantage.
- Ecological cooperation: restoration, sharing, and resilience.
- Technological optimism: research and engineering-led responses.

These are not permanent classes. Do not assign players a fixed morality, psychological diagnosis, or secret personality score. Track actions and their consequences, not a definitive claim about who a person “really is.”

## 4. Inaction states

Represent distinct circumstances:
- Uninformed or information-inaccessible.
- Uncertain because evidence is incomplete.
- Avoidant of evidence that threatens interests or identity.
- Privately aware but publicly silent.
- Unable to act because of poverty, dependency, or credible retaliation.
- Actively obstructing a response.
- Supporting or organizing a response.

Consequences should depend on the player's power, resources, information, and position. Do not equate a powerless player with a leader who knowingly blocks reform.

## 5. Collective-action mechanics

Potential systems:
- Shared watershed, energy, or food-resilience projects.
- Voluntary contributions and free-rider dynamics.
- Public commitments and visible follow-through.
- Worker retraining and transition support.
- Local rules and enforcement.
- Negotiation over who pays for adaptation.
- Scientific reporting and community investigation.
- Whistleblowing with possible social or economic risk.
- Misinformation or lobbying only if they are modeled with clear counterplay and do not reward harassment.

No one mechanism should solve every problem. Projects can fail through underfunding, distrust, poor design, unequal burdens, or delayed implementation.

## 6. AP-inspired inquiry mechanics

Draw inspiration from themes such as ignorance, desire, identification/ego, conditioning, fear, and self-inquiry. Treat these as prompts for reflection, not as a forced belief system.

### Ignorance
Distinguish lack of access to information from avoidance of known evidence. Research should reveal causal relationships that actually exist in the simulation.

### Desire
Ask what the player is seeking—practical utility, comfort, status, competition, habit, or security. Responses should not directly modify the physics. Their importance comes from the decisions that follow.

### Identification
A player's reputation or role may create pressure to defend a prior position. Example: an industrial leader must choose whether to acknowledge evidence that threatens their public identity.

### Conditioning
Different communities may normalize different lifestyles and infrastructure. Players can challenge or reinforce these norms.

### Fear
A community may resist a transition because people fear unemployment, higher prices, or loss of security. Transition support can reduce the burden and improve cooperation.

### Inquiry
Contextual questions may include:
- What problem am I trying to solve?
- Is this addressing a cause or only a symptom?
- What assumptions make this choice feel necessary?
- Am I protecting an identity or following evidence?
- Who benefits and who bears the cost?
- What would change if this habit were questioned?

Prompts should be brief, optional, context-aware, and non-repetitive. Never make the player pass a philosophy quiz to progress.

## 7. Reflection design constraints

- Do not diagnose the player.
- Do not label a player enlightened, egoistic, ignorant, or morally inferior.
- Do not secretly infer real-world sensitive traits.
- Do not reward selecting a “correct” response with magical resource bonuses.
- Do not make AP content a substitute for climate science.
- Clearly distinguish philosophical inspiration from empirical claims.
- Where content directly quotes or closely summarizes a source, handle attribution and permissions appropriately.
- Players must be able to dismiss prompts and continue.
- The game should remain playable for people who disagree with the philosophical framing.

## 8. Institutions and reform

Potential institutions:
- Local council.
- Watershed authority.
- Energy cooperative.
- Public research office.
- Worker/household transition fund.
- Independent environmental monitoring.

Each institution needs:
- A clear authority and scope.
- Resources or enforcement capacity.
- Costs and limitations.
- Possible capture by powerful interests.
- Accountability mechanisms.
- A visible relationship between policy and world state.

Do not implement every institution at once. Add one, test it, and ensure players understand its effect.

## 9. Acceptance criteria

Phase 2 should not begin until Phase 1 acceptance criteria pass. Phase 2 is complete when:
- Social variables have clear definitions and causal rules.
- At least two collective-action situations produce meaningful trade-offs.
- Inaction and active obstruction are distinct.
- Power constraints are represented fairly.
- Reflection prompts are optional and contextual.
- No hidden moral score is used.
- Institutions have clear costs, powers, and limitations.
- Tests cover social-state transitions and reform outcomes.
- UI explains why a social outcome occurred.
