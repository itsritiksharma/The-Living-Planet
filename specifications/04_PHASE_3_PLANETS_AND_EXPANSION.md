# Phase 3 — Multiple Planets, Exploration, and Settlement

## Purpose

This is a future expansion. Do not implement it in the first prototype. Begin only after the local-world simulation and social systems are stable.

## 1. Design objective

Multiple planets should introduce different ecological starting conditions and settlement challenges—not serve as a free escape from the consequences of previous choices.

The philosophical question is:

> Will players reproduce the habits and institutions they carried from the first world, or will they use what they learned to organize life differently?

The game must not imply that collapse is inevitable everywhere. Player decisions, technology, governance, and ecological constraints should produce different outcomes.

## 2. Example fictional worlds

These are gameplay archetypes, not claims about known habitable planets.

### Verdant
- Relatively abundant water and fertile land.
- Main challenge: complacency, overconsumption, and expansion.
- Potential tension: abundance makes limits easy to ignore.

### Arida
- Scarce water and fragile ecosystems.
- Main challenge: allocation, fairness, efficiency, and conflict prevention.
- Potential tension: essential resource access versus profitable use.

### Borealis
- Extreme cold and limited growing seasons.
- Main challenge: energy dependence, heating, and food security.
- Potential tension: short-term energy needs versus long-term ecological and material costs.

### Pelagia
- Ocean-dominated world with vulnerable coastal settlements.
- Main challenge: marine ecosystem stability, storms, coastal planning, and freshwater.
- Potential tension: settlement growth versus coastal and ocean resilience.

Each world must have a distinct set of starting parameters and mechanics. Do not merely reskin the same world.

## 3. Travel and settlement requirements

Reaching a new planet should require:
- Research and technological capacity.
- Energy and material investment.
- Reliable coordination and institutions.
- Transport infrastructure.
- Life-support systems.
- Water recycling and food production.
- Maintenance and spare parts.
- A settlement plan that accounts for the planet's conditions.

The cost must be visible. Do not let players unlock a new planet with a single button or a trivial currency fee.

## 4. Migration and inherited behavior

A new settlement may inherit:
- Production systems.
- Energy technology.
- Institutional structures.
- Social norms.
- Inequality and ownership patterns.
- Lessons from prior environmental failures.
- Habits of resource allocation and consumption.

Some systems can be redesigned. Others create path dependence. Players should be able to carry useful technology and knowledge, but should not automatically escape social and ecological constraints.

## 5. Avoid simplistic planet scores

Do not rank planets solely by a single “habitability” meter. Track relevant conditions separately:
- Water availability and recycling.
- Food-production feasibility.
- Energy requirements.
- Environmental stability.
- Material access.
- Infrastructure resilience.
- Settlement health and social coordination.

A planet may be challenging in one area but manageable in another.

## 6. Acceptance criteria

This phase is complete when:
- At least two planets have meaningfully different mechanics.
- Travel has explicit resource, technology, and coordination costs.
- Settlement systems depend on local environmental conditions.
- A new world does not reset all resource constraints.
- Players can transfer useful knowledge and infrastructure choices.
- The simulation clearly communicates uncertainty and fictional assumptions.
- Tests cover travel costs, settlement prerequisites, and planet-specific rules.
